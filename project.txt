1. WHAT IS THIS PROJECT & WHY DOES IT EXIST?
====================================================================================================

1. What is a "Software Vulnerability" (CVE)?
   Whenever programmers write software (like Windows, Linux, Google Chrome, Apache web servers, or banking 
   apps), they accidentally leave security flaws in the code. When cybersecurity researchers find these 
   flaws, they publish them globally under a standardized identifier called a CVE (Common Vulnerabilities 
   and Exposures), such as "CVE-2021-44228" (Log4Shell).

2. The Problem: "Vulnerability Overload & Analyst Fatigue"
   Every month, over 2,500 brand-new CVEs are published globally!
   A modern bank or enterprise runs tens of thousands of servers. Security teams wake up to hundreds of 
   new alerts daily. A team of 4–5 security engineers CANNOT fix ("patch") every machine every day.
   Attempting to patch everything leads to engineer burnout and causes genuine zero-day threats to be missed.

3. Why Traditional CVSS Scores Fail in Real Life:
   Normally, organizations wait for the government (NIST NVD) to assign a severity score (0 to 10).
   Here is why relying solely on CVSS base scores is dangerous:
     - Case A: A vulnerability has a theoretical score of 9.8/10. BUT hackers don't know how to attack it, 
       no exploit script exists, and the vulnerable software runs on an isolated printer in a locked basement. 
       Dropping everything to patch it today is a waste of corporate resources!
     - Case B: Another vulnerability has an average score of 7.2/10. BUT Russian or ransomware syndicates 
       have weaponized it, automated attack scripts are circulating on GitHub, and it runs on your company's 
       public payment gateway website!
   Which one is the true emergency? Case B! But traditional CVSS says Case A is worse.

4. The "Leakage Trap" in Prior Academic Projects:
   Many student projects trained ML models using CVSS vector submetrics (`AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N...`).
   This was fatal data leakage: the CVSS score is calculated mathematically from those exact submetrics!
   Giving those submetrics to a machine learning model is like giving a student the exam answer key and 
   praising them for scoring 100%. Furthermore, when a CVE is first disclosed, those submetrics DO NOT EXIST yet!

5. What Our Solution Does (Symptom-Based AI Triage Doctor):
   Our platform acts like an emergency room triage doctor guessing illness severity strictly from the patient's 
   symptoms BEFORE the official lab report arrives:
     - Symptom Inputs: Raw description text, CWE weakness taxonomy, vendor/product names, attack keywords.
     - ML Output: Calibrated severity classification (NONE, LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, CRITICAL).
     - Bonus Forecaster: Probability of being added to CISA KEV (exploited in the wild) within 30/90 days.
     - Stage 2 Risk Engine: Combines ML severity with real-time FIRST EPSS exploit probability, CISA KEV catalog, 
       asset business criticality, and internet exposure into a single normalized COMPOSITE RISK SCORE (0 to 100).
     - Actionable SLA:
         * 80.0 – 100.0 (CRITICAL RISK) --> Immediate patch within 24 to 72 Hours!
         * 60.0 – 79.9  (HIGH RISK)     --> Patch within 7 Days!
         * 30.0 – 59.9  (MEDIUM RISK)   --> Scheduled patch within 30 Days.
         * 0.0 – 29.9   (LOW RISK)      --> Routine maintenance / best effort.

3. COMPREHENSIVE DICTIONARY OF ALL MLOPS CONCEPTS & DEFINITIONS
====================================================================================================

1. MLOps (Machine Learning Operations):
   - Definition: The union of Machine Learning, DevOps, and Data Engineering to build, deploy, monitor, 
     and maintain models in production reliably, automatically, and reproducibly.

2. Feature Leakage (Target Leakage & Temporal Leakage):
   - Target Leakage: When features derived from or correlated with the target label are fed into the model.
   - In our system: We removed all 22 CVSS submetrics from Stage 1. We also banned EPSS scores and KEV flags 
     from Stage 1 because they are assigned post-publication.

3. DVC (Data Version Control):
   - Definition: Git for data. Tracks multi-gigabyte datasets, feature matrices, and trained models using 
     content-addressed hash files (`dvc.lock`, `.dvc/config`), enabling reproducible pipelines via `dvc repro`.

4. MLflow Model Registry Aliases (@champion & @challenger):
   - Modern replacement for deprecated MLflow "Stages" (Production/Staging). Models are assigned dynamic 
     tags (`@champion` = production server model; `@challenger` = newly retrained candidate under evaluation).

5. Population Stability Index (PSI):
   - A statistical metric measuring how much an inference feature distribution has drifted away from the 
     original training baseline distribution across 10 quantile deciles.

6. Text Centroid Cosine Distance Drift:
   - Tracking semantic drift in incoming vulnerability descriptions by measuring the cosine distance between 
     the centroid of production text TF-IDF embeddings and the training baseline centroid.

7. Late Ground Truth Labels:
   - When models make predictions on Day 0, but NIST human analysts publish the official CVSS ground-truth 
     label 14 to 30 days later. Our system automatically joins delayed labels to calculate live rolling Macro F1.

8. Balanced Sample Class Weights vs. SMOTE:
   - SMOTE generates synthetic points between nearest neighbors. On sparse, high-dimensional text representations 
     (TF-IDF n-grams + boolean flags), SMOTE creates nonsensical, noisy word vectors.
   - Class Weights dynamically penalize minority class errors during loss calculation without hallucinating fake data.

6. STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN: WHAT WE HAVE DONE & HOW WE DID IT
====================================================================================================

PHASE 1: REAL-WORLD INGESTION (7,157 REAL NVD CVEs)
---------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  - Ingested 7,157 real-world CVEs from 2019 to 2024 into `data/raw/nvd_cves_recent.csv`.
  - Downloaded live daily FIRST EPSS bulk probability scores into `epss_history`.
  - Synced the official CISA KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) catalog.
  - Reserved `seed_data.py` exclusively for isolated unit test mock suites.

PHASE 2: DATABASE DESIGN & STRICT LEAKAGE GUARD
------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  - Created `docker/postgres/init.sql` and `src/serving/api/db.py` with append-only tables:
    * `predictions_log`: Captures every real-time inference request, description text, CWE, and score.
    * `analyst_feedback`: Captures security analyst ground-truth corrections.
  - Implemented an automatic 30-second failure cooldown to ensure seamless offline degradation when 
    running without Docker.

PHASE 3: ZERO-LEAKAGE SYMPTOM FEATURE ENGINEERING
-------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  - Completely stripped all 22 CVSS v3.1 vector submetrics (`AV`, `AC`, `PR`, `UI`, `S`, `C`, `I`, `A`).
  - Feature matrix is built purely from disclosure symptoms:
    * 100 TF-IDF n-grams extracted from raw CVE descriptions.
    * 10 binary attack mechanism flags: RCE, SQLi, Buffer Overflow, Auth Bypass, SSRF, XSS, Path Traversal, 
      Privilege Escalation, Command Injection, Memory Corruption.
    * CWE parent weakness category mappings.
    * Affected vendor/product counts & reference link counts.
  - Guaranteed zero leakage: `epss_score`, `in_cisa_kev`, and `cvss_score` are strictly banned from $X$.

PHASE 4: STAGE 1 ML CLASSIFIER & CLASS WEIGHT BALANCING
-------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  - Conducted empirical balancing comparison: Unweighted vs SMOTE vs Balanced Class Weights.
  - Demonstrated that SMOTE damages high-dimensional sparse text representations.
  - Integrated `compute_sample_weight("balanced")` directly into LightGBM and XGBoost loss functions.
  - Calibrated class probabilities using `CalibratedClassifierCV` (isotonic regression).

7. MATHEMATICAL FORMULAS & WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLES
====================================================================================================

FORMULA 1: COMPOSITE RISK SCORE CALCULATION
--------------------------------------------
Formula:
  RiskScore = 100 * [ (0.30 * S_CVSS) + (0.25 * S_KEV) + (0.20 * S_EPSS) + (0.15 * S_Asset) + (0.07 * S_Exposure) + (0.03 * S_Confidence) ]

Numerical Example:
  Suppose a newly disclosed vulnerability:
    * Calibrated ML predicted severity: S_CVSS = 0.72
    * EPSS exploit likelihood:           S_EPSS = 0.55
    * Listed in active CISA KEV catalog: S_KEV = 1.00
    * High-value database server:        S_Asset = 0.80
    * Internet-facing system:            S_Exposure = 1.00
    * Model posterior probability:       S_Confidence = 0.85

  Step 1: Weighted components:
    - Base Severity:  0.30 * 0.72 = 0.2160
    - CISA KEV:       0.25 * 1.00 = 0.2500
    - FIRST EPSS:     0.20 * 0.55 = 0.1100
    - Asset Rating:   0.15 * 0.80 = 0.1200
    - Exposure:       0.07 * 1.00 = 0.0700
    - Confidence:     0.03 * 0.85 = 0.0255

  Step 2: Sum & scale:
    Sum = 0.2160 + 0.2500 + 0.1100 + 0.1200 + 0.0700 + 0.0255 = 0.7915
    Risk Score = 100 * 0.7915 = 79.15
    SLA Tier: HIGH_RISK (Remediate within 7 Days).

8. COMPREHENSIVE VIVA & EXAMINER QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS (WHAT SIR WILL ASK)
====================================================================================================

Q1: Why did you eliminate CVSS vector submetrics (AV, AC, PR, UI, S, C, I, A) from your ML model?
Answer:
  "Sir, because training on CVSS vector features is textbook data leakage. The CVSS base score is calculated 
   using a known, closed-form formula from those exact vectors. If we give those vectors to a model, it does 
   not learn vulnerability semantics; it simply memorizes the calculator formula. More importantly, when a 
   CVE is disclosed on Day 0, official CVSS vectors DO NOT EXIST yet—they take weeks for NIST analysts to publish. 
   Our model predicts severity strictly from disclosure symptoms: raw description text, CWE, and vendor."

Q2: Why did you use Balanced Class Weights instead of SMOTE?
Answer:
  "Sir, while SMOTE works well for continuous low-dimensional data, our feature matrix consists of 100 sparse 
   TF-IDF n-grams and binary keyword flags. SMOTE creates synthetic instances by linear interpolation between 
   neighbors. On sparse text, this generates impossible combinations—hallucinating fractional word frequencies 
   and contradictory keyword co-occurrences. Class weighting adjusts the gradient penalty in the loss function 
   without distorting the authentic, sparse geometry of the feature space."

Q3: What baseline models did you compare against, and what were the findings?
Answer:
  "Sir, we benchmarked a 5-stage hierarchy on an identical real-world holdout test set:
   1. Majority Class Dummy: Macro F1 = 0.1681.
   2. Keyword Rules: Macro F1 = 0.1441. High critical recall (0.54) but horrible calibration (ECE = 0.7125) 
      causing extreme false-positive alert fatigue.
   3. Logistic Regression: Macro F1 = 0.2019.
   4. XGBoost (Calibrated): Macro F1 = 0.2970, ECE = 0.1291.
   5. LightGBM (Class Weighted): Macro F1 = 0.3082, ECE = 0.1468.
   Tree-based ensembles with class weights significantly outperformed heuristics across both macro and weighted F1."

Q4: What is DVC, and why did you use it alongside Git?
Answer:
  "Sir, Git is designed for code and cannot track large datasets or binary model files efficiently. 
   We used DVC (Data Version Control) to codify the pipeline into 5 reproducible stages (ingest, validate, 
   features, train, evaluate) in `dvc.yaml`. When raw data changes, running `dvc repro` executes only the affected 
   stages, ensuring complete data and model lineage reproducibility."

Q5: What are MLflow Aliases, and why did you use them instead of Stages?
Answer:
  "Sir, in MLflow 2.8+, Stages (Production, Staging, Archived) were formally deprecated. 
   Modern MLOps best practice uses mutable Aliases like `@champion` and `@challenger`. 
   Our automated quality gate promotes models by assigning the `@champion` alias, and our FastAPI server dynamically 
   loads `@champion` without downtime."

Q6: How do you detect drift when ground-truth labels arrive weeks later?
Answer:
  "Sir, we address this in two layers:
   1. Input Data Drift (Immediate): We monitor Population Stability Index (PSI) on numerical features, 
      text centroid cosine distance on TF-IDF embeddings, and Chi-Square goodness-of-fit on CWE categories 
      from PostgreSQL `predictions_log`.
   2. Late Ground Truth Evaluation (Delayed): When official NIST labels are eventually published, our 
      `late_labels_evaluator.py` joins historical predictions to compute rolling production Macro F1."

Q7: How did you benchmark API latency and throughput?
Answer:
  "Sir, we executed `scripts/simulate_traffic.py --count 100` against the live FastAPI endpoint. 
   Under load, our median p50 latency was 14.59 ms, p95 was 19.14 ms, p99 was 100.81 ms, with a 100% success rate, 
   comfortably meeting enterprise SLA requirements (<50 ms median)."
