PART A: THE REAL-WORLD CYBERSECURITY PROBLEM (Why are we building this?)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. What is a "Software Vulnerability" (CVE)?
   Whenever programmers write software (like Windows, Linux, Google Chrome, Apache servers, or banking 
   apps), they accidentally leave security bugs in the code. When cybersecurity researchers find these 
   flaws, they publish them globally under a standardized code name called a CVE (Common Vulnerabilities 
   and Exposures), such as "CVE-2024-3094".

2. The Problem: "Vulnerability Overload / Fatigue"
   Every single month, over 2,500 brand-new CVEs are published globally!
   Now put yourself in the shoes of a Security Engineer working at a large company (like a bank):
   Your company runs 10,000 servers and laptops. Every morning, you wake up to an email alert listing 
   hundreds of new software vulnerabilities affecting your servers.
   You have a team of only 4 security engineers. You CANNOT fix ("patch") hundreds of servers every day. 
   If you try to patch everything, your team burns out and the real critical servers get ignored.

3. Why Existing Solutions (CVSS Scores) Fail in Real Life:
   Normally, the government (NIST) assigns a severity score from 0 to 10 called the CVSS score.
   Here is why relying ONLY on CVSS score is a disaster for companies:
     - Case 1: A vulnerability has a huge theoretical score of 9.8/10. BUT hackers don't even know 
       how to attack it, nobody wrote an exploit script, and the machine running that software is an 
       isolated printer in the basement with zero internet connection. Fixing it today is a waste of time!
     - Case 2: Another vulnerability has an average score of 7.2/10. BUT Russian or North Korean hacker 
       gangs have weaponized it, automated hacking scripts are circulating on GitHub, and it runs on 
       your company's public payment gateway website! 
   Which one is the REAL danger? Case 2! But traditional CVSS tells you Case 1 is worse.

4. What Our Solution Does:
   Our system acts like an "Automated AI Triage Doctor".
   It ingests the raw vulnerability, uses Machine Learning to predict how dangerous the bug is technically, 
   checks global threat intelligence (EPSS & CISA KEV) to see if hackers are actually exploiting it in 
   the real world right now, checks how critical your internal server is, and calculates a single 
   COMPOSITE RISK SCORE (0 to 100) with a strict action deadline:
     - Score 80-100: CRITICAL RISK --> Fix within 24 to 72 Hours!
     - Score 60-79:  HIGH RISK     --> Fix within 7 Days!
     - Score 30-59:  MEDIUM RISK   --> Fix in next monthly maintenance (30 Days).
     - Score 0-29:   LOW RISK      --> Ignore or fix when free.

PART B: WHY "MLOPS" INSTEAD OF JUST A NORMAL JUPYTER NOTEBOOK?
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In college, Machine Learning is usually taught like this:
  `df = pd.read_csv("data.csv")`
  `model.fit(X_train, y_train)`
  `print("Accuracy:", 95%)`
  ...and you close your laptop.

In a real company, that Jupyter Notebook is COMPLETELY USELESS on day 2. Why?
  1. Where does tomorrow's data come from? (Someone has to manually download new CSVs every day?)
  2. How do other developers use your model? (They can't open your `.ipynb` file).
  3. What happens when hackers change their tactics? (Your model's accuracy drops from 95% to 50% 
     in 3 months because the real world changed—this is called "Data Drift").
  4. Who monitors if the model is alive, fast, or failing?

MLOps (Machine Learning Operations) solves this by building the entire AUTOMATED FACTORY around the model:

  1. AUTOMATED DATA PIPELINE (Apache Airflow):
     Instead of humans downloading files, Airflow runs scheduled cron scripts every day that automatically 
     connect to cybersecurity APIs, download new CVE records, and store them in a PostgreSQL database.

  2. MODEL EXPERIMENT TRACKING & REGISTRY (MLflow):
     Every time we train a model, MLflow tracks the code version, hyperparameters, accuracy, and saves 
     the actual trained model binary (`model.pkl`). It maintains a "Champion" (current production model) 
     and evaluates new "Challengers".

  3. PRODUCTION SERVING REST API (FastAPI):
     We wrap the model in a high-speed web server. Any website, mobile app, or security tool can send 
     a JSON request to `http://localhost:8000/api/v1/risk-score` and get back the predicted risk in 5 milliseconds.

  4. REAL-TIME OBSERVABILITY (Prometheus + Grafana):
     Prometheus continuously tracks how many requests are being handled, how slow or fast the model is, 
     and Grafana draws live graphs on screen that update automatically.

  5. AUTOMATED SELF-HEALING & RETRAINING (Continuous Monitoring):
     Every 6 hours, our system mathematically checks if the incoming vulnerabilities look different from 
     the original training data (using statistical tests: KS-Test & PSI).
     If the data has drifted significantly (>= 25% features changed), the system DOES NOT panic or wait 
     for a human—Airflow AUTOMATICALLY starts a retraining pipeline, trains a new model, checks if it beats 
     the old one, and promotes it to production live!

In short: 
Machine Learning is just the "Brain". 
MLOps is the entire "Body" (skeleton, nervous system, blood circulation, and immune system) that keeps 
the brain alive and working 24/7 without dying.

3. COMPREHENSIVE DICTIONARY OF ALL MLOPS CONCEPTS & DEFINITIONS
====================================================================================================

1. MLOps (Machine Learning Operations):
   - Definition: The combination of Machine Learning, DevOps, and Data Engineering to build, deploy, 
     monitor, and maintain ML models in production reliably and automatically.
   - Analogy: If building an ML model in a Jupyter Notebook is like baking a single cake at home, 
     MLOps is designing the automated, clean, temperature-controlled factory that bakes 10,000 cakes 
     a day without burning any of them.

2. CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures):
   - Definition: A standardized international identifier (e.g., CVE-2024-3094) assigned to a publicly 
     disclosed cybersecurity flaw so that security professionals across the globe refer to the same bug.

3. CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System):
   - Definition: An industry standard (v3.1) that provides a technical vector string 
     (e.g., CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H) and a base score from 0.0 to 10.0 describing 
     the intrinsic severity of a flaw.

4. EPSS (Exploit Prediction Scoring System):
   - Definition: A daily-updated model from FIRST.org that outputs a probability (between 0.0 and 1.0) 
     estimating whether a vulnerability will be actively exploited by hackers in the wild within 30 days.

5. CISA KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog):
   - Definition: An official catalog maintained by the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security 
     Agency (CISA) listing vulnerabilities with confirmed real-world weaponization and active attacks.

6. Data Leakage (Target Leakage & Temporal Leakage):
   - Definition: When information from outside the training dataset (or from the future) is accidentally 
     used to train the machine learning model, causing it to look unrealistically accurate in testing 
     but fail miserably in production.
   - In our project: EPSS scores and KEV flags are added DAYS or MONTHS after a CVE is published. 
     If we included EPSS/KEV inside our Stage 1 ML model, the model would cheat by "looking into the future". 
     We strictly isolated these features to Stage 2 to guarantee zero leakage!

7. Append-Only Storage Architecture:
   - Definition: A database design where historical rows are NEVER overwritten or updated; new records 
     are only appended with timestamps.
   - Why we used it: Allows exact point-in-time historical reconstruction. When evaluating how our model 
     would have performed on March 1st, 2024, we only query threat intelligence that existed on that day.

8. CVSS Vector Decomposition (One-Hot Binary Flags):
   - Definition: Breaking down a raw cryptographic-looking CVSS string into 22 distinct binary (0 or 1) 
     features representing Attack Vector, Complexity, Privileges, User Interaction, and CIA Impacts.

9. Class Imbalance & SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique):
   - Definition: When some output classes have far fewer samples than others. In security datasets, 
     CRITICAL and HIGH bugs are abundant, while NONE and LOW bugs are rare.
   - SMOTE creates synthetic (mathematically generated) examples of the rare classes so the ML model 
     learns to recognize all classes fairly instead of guessing "HIGH" every time.

10. Multi-Class Classification & XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting):
    - Definition: Predicting one label out of more than two classes (NONE, LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, CRITICAL).
    - XGBoost: An ensemble of decision trees where each new tree corrects the errors made by previous 
      trees. It provides state-of-the-art accuracy on tabular data with sub-10ms inference speed.

11. Probability Calibration (Isotonic Regression):
    - Definition: Raw machine learning classifiers often output overconfident or distorted probability 
      scores. Isotonic Regression fits a non-parametric monotonic function to transform raw logits into 
      true empirical probabilities (e.g. if the model outputs 0.80, exactly 80% of those cases are true).

12. Explainable AI (XAI) & SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations):
    - Definition: Based on cooperative game theory, SHAP calculates the exact positive or negative 
      mathematical contribution of every single input feature to the final prediction.
    - Analogy: In a football match won 3-0, SHAP tells you exactly how many goals were contributed by 
      the striker, the midfielder, and the goalkeeper.

13. Two-Stage Decision Architecture:
    - Stage 1: Machine Learning Classifier (Predicts purely technical severity from bug characteristics).
    - Stage 2: Deterministic Business Risk Engine (Combines ML + EPSS + KEV + Asset Criticality + Internet Exposure).

14. Experiment Tracking & Artifacts (MLflow):
    - Definition: A dedicated platform that logs hyperparameters (learning rate, depth), performance 
      metrics (Precision, Recall, F1), code versions, and saved model binaries (`model.pkl`) for every run.

15. Model Registry & Champion vs. Challenger Quality Gate:
    - Definition: The Model Registry is the centralized store of versioned models.
    - Champion: The active model currently serving live traffic in production.
    - Challenger: A freshly retrained model candidate.
    - Quality Gate: An automated test. The Challenger is only promoted if its Macro F1 score beats 
      the Champion by at least 0.01 (+1%). If it fails, the Champion remains active.

16. Model Serving & REST API (FastAPI):
    - Definition: Exposing the trained model via lightweight HTTP network endpoints (like `/predict` 
      and `/risk-score`) so external applications, web dashboards, and security tools can ask for predictions.

17. Observability & Telemetry (Prometheus & Grafana):
    - Prometheus: A time-series database that pulls ("scrapes") numerical metrics (request count, latency, 
      drift share) from our application every few seconds.
    - Grafana: A visualization dashboard that queries Prometheus to render real-time charts and gauges.

18. Data Drift vs. Concept Drift:
    - Data Drift (Covariate Shift): The distribution of the INPUT features changes over time 
      (e.g., suddenly 80% of new vulnerabilities affect Cloud/Kubernetes instead of Windows).
    - Concept Drift: The statistical relationship between inputs and outputs changes 
      (e.g., a bug that used to be rated MEDIUM is now considered CRITICAL because new hacking tools exist).

19. Statistical Distance Metrics (KS-Test & PSI):
    - KS-Test (Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test): A non-parametric test comparing the continuous cumulative 
      distributions of two datasets to check if they come from the same population (p-value < 0.05 indicates drift).
    - PSI (Population Stability Index): A binned metric measuring how much a variable has shifted:
        * PSI < 0.10: No significant shift (Stable).
        * 0.10 <= PSI <= 0.25: Moderate shift (Warning).
        * PSI > 0.25: Significant distributional shift (Drift Alert!).

20. Workflow Orchestration (Apache Airflow):
    - Definition: A platform to programmatically author, schedule, and monitor workflows as Directed 
      Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) of tasks with automatic retry handling, branching, and alerting.

5. STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN: WHAT WE HAVE DONE & HOW WE DID IT
====================================================================================================

PHASE 1: DATA INGESTION & AUTHORITATIVE SECURITY FEEDS
------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  We implemented three automated Python ingestion clients in `src/ingestion/`:
  1. `NVDClient`: Connects to NIST NVD 2.0 REST API. Pulls CVE IDs, descriptions, CWE identifiers, 
     affected CPE software configurations, and raw CVSS v3.1 vector strings.
  2. `EPSSClient`: Downloads the daily gzipped bulk CSV archive from FIRST.org containing exploit 
     probability scores for all 250,000+ known CVEs.
  3. `CISAKEVClient`: Fetches the official JSON catalog of weaponized exploits actively used by cybercriminals.
  4. `AssetMatcher`: Reads internal enterprise asset inventory to link vulnerabilities to physical/virtual machines.

PHASE 2: DATABASE DESIGN & STRICT LEAKAGE GUARD
------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  We created `docker/postgres/init.sql` with a schema specifically engineered to eliminate temporal leakage:
  - `cves` table: Stores static vulnerability information.
  - `epss_history` table: Strictly APPEND-ONLY table storing `(cve_id, epss_score, snapshot_date)`.
  - `kev_history` table: Strictly APPEND-ONLY table storing `(cve_id, date_added_to_kev)`.
  - `asset_inventory` table: Stores `asset_id`, criticality (1 to 5), and `is_internet_facing` (boolean).
  - Why this matters: Traditional databases overwrite old values. Our append-only design allows the ML 
    pipeline to query what the world looked like on any exact historical date, preventing the model from 
    cheating with future data during training.

PHASE 3: DATA VALIDATION & LEAKAGE-FREE FEATURE ENGINEERING
-----------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  1. Pandera Schema Validation (`src/validation/`):
     - Validates incoming data types, enforces score ranges (0.0 to 10.0 for CVSS, 0.0 to 1.0 for EPSS), 
       and verifies CVE regex patterns (`CVE-\d{4}-\d{4,}`). Invalid rows are quarantined.
  2. CVSS Vector Decomposition (`src/features/cvss_parser.py`):
     - Parses raw CVSS v3.1 strings into 22 explicit one-hot binary flags:
       * Attack Vector (4 flags): Network, Adjacent, Local, Physical
       * Attack Complexity (2 flags): Low, High
       * Privileges Required (3 flags): None, Low, High
       * User Interaction (2 flags): None, Required
       * Scope (2 flags): Unchanged, Changed
       * Confidentiality Impact (3 flags): None, Low, High
       * Integrity Impact (3 flags): None, Low, High
       * Availability Impact (3 flags): None, Low, High
  3. Description NLP Feature Extraction (`src/features/feature_engineer.py`):
     - Scans raw text with compiled regex patterns to detect critical attack types: `remote_execution`, 
       `sql_injection`, `buffer_overflow`, `privilege_escalation`, `cross_site_scripting`.
  4. Strict Leakage Separation:
     - Stage 1 ML ALLOWED: 22 CVSS metric flags, CWE category, description keywords, vendor counts.
     - Stage 1 ML FORBIDDEN: `epss_score`, `epss_percentile`, `kev_flag`, `cvss_score`, `cvss_severity`.

PHASE 4: STAGE 1 ML SEVERITY CLASSIFIER TRAINING
-------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  Implemented in `src/training/train_stage1.py` and `src/models/severity_classifier.py`:
  1. Temporal Train/Test Split: Split data strictly by `published_date` (e.g., train on historical 
     vulnerabilities, test on subsequent years). This simulates true production deployment.
  2. SMOTE Oversampling: Applied SMOTE to the training set to synthesize minority class samples 
     (NONE and LOW) without distorting the real distribution of the test set.
  3. XGBoost Multi-Class Classifier: Trained extreme gradient boosted decision trees with multi-class 
     log-loss objective (`multi:softprob`).
  4. Isotonic Calibration: Calibrated raw output probabilities using `CalibratedClassifierCV` 
     (isotonic regression) so that output confidence scores reflect true empirical probabilities.
  5. MLflow Tracking: Logged all parameters (n_estimators, max_depth, learning_rate), metrics 
     (Macro F1, Precision, Recall, Accuracy), and serialized model artifacts directly into MLflow.

PHASE 5: EXPLAINABLE AI WITH SHAP
----------------------------------
What We Did:
  Implemented in `src/models/explainer.py`:
  - Utilized `shap.TreeExplainer` optimized for XGBoost decision tree ensembles.
  - Generates exact Shapley values showing how much each individual CVSS flag (e.g., `AttackVector_Network=1` 
    or `PrivilegesRequired_None=1`) pushed the prediction towards CRITICAL or down towards LOW.
  - Exposed via `/api/v1/explain` so security analysts can verify WHY the AI made a decision.

PHASE 6: STAGE 2 COMPOSITE RISK SCORING ENGINE
-----------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  Implemented in `src/models/risk_scorer.py`:
  Takes the Stage 1 ML output and blends it with real-time operational context using a mathematically 
  grounded formula:
  
  Risk Score = 100 * [ (0.30 * S_ML) + (0.25 * S_EPSS) + (0.25 * S_KEV) + (0.15 * S_Asset) + (0.05 * S_Exposure) ]
  
  Mapped to Remediation SLAs:
    - 80.0 to 100.0 (CRITICAL RISK) --> SLA: Remediate / Patch within 24 to 72 Hours
    - 60.0 to 79.9  (HIGH RISK)     --> SLA: Remediate / Patch within 7 Days
    - 30.0 to 59.9  (MEDIUM RISK)   --> SLA: Remediate / Patch within 30 Days
    - 0.0 to 29.9   (LOW RISK)      --> SLA: Routine Maintenance / Best Effort

PHASE 7: MODEL SERVING WITH FASTAPI
-----------------------------------
What We Did:
  Built high-throughput, async REST API in `src/serving/api/main.py`:
  - `POST /api/v1/predict`: Accepts vulnerability features, returns predicted severity & calibrated probabilities.
  - `POST /api/v1/risk-score`: Accepts vulnerability & asset context, returns Composite Risk Score & SLA.
  - `POST /api/v1/explain`: Returns SHAP waterfall contribution values for the prediction.
  - `GET /api/v1/drift`: Returns current statistical drift status across all monitored features.
  - `GET /metrics`: Standard Prometheus metrics endpoint.
  - `GET /health`: Kubernetes-ready readiness and liveness probe.

PHASE 8: OBSERVABILITY & TELEMETRY (PROMETHEUS + GRAFANA)
----------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  1. `metrics_exporter.py` in `src/monitoring/`:
     - `vulnerability_prediction_requests_total` (Counter): Total requests received.
     - `vulnerability_prediction_duration_seconds` (Histogram): Request latency buckets (0.01s to 2.5s).
     - `vulnerability_severity_predictions_total` (Counter): Breakdown of predictions by severity.
     - `vulnerability_champion_model_version` (Gauge): Version of the active MLflow model.
     - `vulnerability_data_drift_share` (Gauge): Current feature drift share percentage.
  2. Grafana Dashboard (`prom-demo/grafana_dashboard.json`):
     - Configured panels showing Request Rate, P95 Latency, Severity Distribution, and Drift Alerts.
     - Auto-refresh configured (5s or 10s) to visualize incoming production traffic in real time.

PHASE 9: AUTOMATED STATISTICAL DRIFT MONITORING
------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  Implemented in `src/monitoring/drift_detector.py` and `configs/drift_config.yaml`:
  - Continuously compares baseline training data (`features.parquet`) against production inference logs (`recent_cves.parquet`).
  - For Numerical Features (`epss_score`, `cvss_exploitability_score`, `description_length`, etc.):
      * Computes Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test (p-value threshold = 0.05).
      * Computes Population Stability Index (PSI threshold = 0.20).
  - For Categorical Features (`attack_vector`, `attack_complexity`, `privileges_required`):
      * Computes Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test ($\chi^2$).
  - System Drift Rule:
      * $\text{Drift Share} = \frac{\text{Number of Drifted Features}}{\text{Total Features Tested}}$
      * If $\text{Drift Share} \ge 0.25$ (25%), flags `dataset_drift = True` and calls for retraining!

PHASE 10: AUTOMATED ORCHESTRATION WITH APACHE AIRFLOW
------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  Authored 3 production DAGs in `airflow/dags/`:
  1. `data_ingestion_dag`: Daily scheduled pipeline pulling incremental updates from NVD, EPSS, and KEV.
  2. `drift_monitoring_dag`: Runs every 6 hours (`0 */6 * * *`). Uses a `BranchPythonOperator`:
     - Compares production logs with baseline data.
     - If stable: Routes to `no_drift_detected` task (pipeline completes).
     - If drift detected: Routes to `trigger_retraining_dag` via `TriggerDagRunOperator`.
  3. `model_training_dag`: The retraining pipeline triggered when drift occurs.

PHASE 11: CHAMPION vs. CHALLENGER QUALITY GATE & PROMOTION
-----------------------------------------------------------
What We Did:
  Implemented in `src/training/promotion.py`:
  - When the retraining DAG finishes, it produces a new "Challenger" model.
  - The Quality Gate compares the Challenger against the active "Champion" on a held-out benchmark set:
      * Condition: $\text{Macro F1}_{\text{Challenger}} - \text{Macro F1}_{\text{Champion}} \ge 0.01$ (+1%).
  - If Passed: The Challenger is automatically promoted to "Production" in MLflow Model Registry, 
    and the old Champion is archived.
  - If Failed: The Challenger is rejected, the current Champion stays in production, and an alert is logged.
  - Automated Rollback: If any fatal serving errors occur, the system can instantly roll back to 
    the previous archived Champion version.

6. MATHEMATICAL FORMULAS & WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLES
====================================================================================================

FORMULA 1: COMPOSITE RISK SCORE CALCULATION
--------------------------------------------
Formula:
  RiskScore = 100 * [ (0.30 * S_ML) + (0.25 * S_EPSS) + (0.25 * S_KEV) + (0.15 * S_Asset) + (0.05 * S_Exposure) ]

Weights Justification:
  - S_ML (30%): Intrinsic technical severity of the vulnerability.
  - S_EPSS (25%): Real-world statistical likelihood of exploitation in the next 30 days.
  - S_KEV (25%): Active weaponization in the wild (binary 1.0 or 0.0).
  - S_Asset (15%): Importance of the host server to company operations (1 to 5 normalized to 0.2 to 1.0).
  - S_Exposure (5%): Whether the server is internet-accessible (1.0) or internal (0.0).

Step-by-Step Numerical Example:
  Suppose an attacker discovers a flaw affecting a database server:
    * Stage 1 ML Model predicts HIGH severity with confidence S_ML = 0.72
    * EPSS score indicates a 55% exploit probability: S_EPSS = 0.55
    * CISA has added this CVE to the active KEV catalog: S_KEV = 1.00
    * The affected server is critical (Tier 4 out of 5): S_Asset = 0.80
    * The server is directly accessible from the internet: S_Exposure = 1.00

  Step 1: Calculate weighted components
    - ML Component:       0.30 * 0.72 = 0.2160
    - EPSS Component:     0.25 * 0.55 = 0.1375
    - KEV Component:      0.25 * 1.00 = 0.2500
    - Asset Component:    0.15 * 0.80 = 0.1200
    - Exposure Component: 0.05 * 1.00 = 0.0500

  Step 2: Sum the components
    Sum = 0.2160 + 0.1375 + 0.2500 + 0.1200 + 0.0500 = 0.7735

  Step 3: Scale to 100
    Risk Score = 100 * 0.7735 = 77.35

  Step 4: Map to SLA Tier
    77.35 falls squarely into HIGH_RISK (60.0 to 79.9).
    Action Required: Mandatory remediation / security patch deployed within 7 Days.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FORMULA 2: POPULATION STABILITY INDEX (PSI) CALCULATION
-------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
  PSI = SUM [ (Actual% - Expected%) * ln(Actual% / Expected%) ]

Calculation Procedure:
  1. Divide the baseline training distribution (Expected) into 10 quantile bins (deciles).
  2. Count how many samples from the recent production distribution (Actual) fall into each bin.
  3. Convert sample counts into percentages (proportions of total).
  4. For each bin, calculate: (Actual_pct - Expected_pct) * ln(Actual_pct / Expected_pct).
  5. Sum across all 10 bins.

Interpretation Thresholds:
  - PSI < 0.10: Distributions are virtually identical. No action needed.
  - 0.10 <= PSI <= 0.25: Slight distribution shift. System generates an informational warning.
  - PSI > 0.25: Substantial statistical shift! System flags drift and triggers automated retraining.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FORMULA 3: KOLMOGOROV-SMIRNOV (KS-TEST) STATISTIC
-------------------------------------------------
Formula:
  D = sup_x | F_reference(x) - F_current(x) |

Explanation:
  $D$ is the maximum vertical distance between the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the 
  baseline reference data and the CDF of the current production data. If the resulting p-value 
  is less than 0.05 ($p < 0.05$), we reject the null hypothesis and declare that the distribution has drifted.

7. COMPREHENSIVE VIVA & EXAMINER QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS (WHAT SIR WILL ASK)
====================================================================================================

Q1: Why do we need Machine Learning here at all? Why can't we just use the CVSS score published by NIST?
Answer:
  "Sir, there are two critical reasons:
   First, when a brand-new vulnerability is discovered, it often takes NIST analysts several days or 
   weeks to assign an official CVSS score. During that window, organizations are vulnerable. Our Stage 1 
   ML model predicts the technical severity instantly from the vulnerability's initial text description 
   and metric vector flags.
   Second, CVSS base score only measures theoretical technical severity in a vacuum. It does NOT consider 
   whether hackers have weaponized the bug (EPSS/KEV) or whether the affected computer is a mission-critical 
   server facing the public internet. Our system solves this by combining ML predictions with threat intelligence 
   and asset context into a single actionable risk score."

Q2: What is Data Leakage, and how did you prevent it in this project?
Answer:
  "Sir, data leakage occurs when future or target information is accidentally made available to a machine 
   learning model during training. In our project, there are two distinct types of leakage that we guarded against:
   1. Target Leakage: Threat intelligence metrics like EPSS (exploit probability) and CISA KEV (known exploited catalog) 
      are created DAYS or MONTHS after a vulnerability is published. If we put EPSS or KEV into the Stage 1 ML model, 
      the model would cheat by looking into the future. We strictly banned EPSS, KEV, and CVSS scores from the ML feature matrix. 
      They are ONLY introduced in Stage 2 mathematical risk scoring.
   2. Temporal Leakage: Instead of doing a random train_test_split (which mixes future vulnerabilities with past ones), 
      we split the dataset strictly along the time dimension based on published_date, ensuring the model is evaluated 
      only on vulnerabilities published AFTER the training window."

Q3: Why did you choose XGBoost instead of a Deep Learning Neural Network or Large Language Model (LLM)?
Answer:
  "Sir, we selected XGBoost for three engineering reasons:
   1. Tabular Efficiency: The inputs to our Stage 1 model are 22 binary CVSS flags, categorical CWE IDs, 
      and keyword indicators. For structured tabular data, gradient boosted decision trees consistently 
      outperform neural networks in empirical benchmarks.
   2. Inference Latency: In production, our model must evaluate incoming vulnerabilities in real time. 
      XGBoost executes in under 10 milliseconds, whereas an LLM or deep transformer would require GPU infrastructure 
      and take hundreds of milliseconds.
   3. Explainability: XGBoost integrates natively with SHAP TreeExplainer, allowing us to compute exact 
      mathematical feature attribution for every single prediction."

Q4: Why was Isotonic Regression used after training the XGBoost classifier?
Answer:
  "Sir, standard classifiers like XGBoost optimize for separation and classification accuracy, but their raw output 
   scores are not well-calibrated true probabilities (for instance, a raw confidence of 0.8 might not correspond to 
   an empirical 80% likelihood). Because our Stage 2 Composite Risk formula uses the ML confidence score as a 
   mathematical weight, that score MUST represent a calibrated probability. Isotonic Regression fits a monotonic 
   mapping function to calibrate the model's raw logits into true probabilities."

Q5: What is the difference between Data Drift and Concept Drift? How does your project detect them?
Answer:
  "Sir:
   - Data Drift (Covariate Shift) occurs when the distribution of the input features changes over time. 
     For example, if attackers shift from memory corruption bugs to cloud API misconfigurations, the frequency 
     of specific CVSS attack vectors changes.
   - Concept Drift occurs when the relationship between the inputs and the output target changes. 
     For example, a vulnerability type previously deemed LOW severity becomes CRITICAL because a novel 
     exploitation technique was invented.
   In our platform, we detect Data Drift using the Population Stability Index (PSI) and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS-test) 
   in `drift_detector.py`. If the drift share across tested features exceeds 25%, the system automatically flags drift."

Q6: How does the Automated Retraining loop work end-to-end?
Answer:
  "Sir, the entire retraining feedback loop is orchestrated by Apache Airflow:
   1. The `drift_monitoring_dag` runs every 6 hours on a cron schedule (`0 */6 * * *`).
   2. It compares the baseline training dataset (`features.parquet`) with recent production logs (`recent_cves.parquet`).
   3. A `BranchPythonOperator` evaluates the drift share.
   4. If drift share < 25%, the pipeline logs that the system is stable and terminates.
   5. If drift share >= 25%, the operator branches to `trigger_retraining_dag`.
   6. Airflow triggers the `vulnerability_model_training_pipeline`, which ingests fresh data, retrains a new 
      Challenger model, logs artifacts to MLflow, and submits the Challenger to the Quality Gate."

Q7: What happens if the freshly retrained model is WORSE than the existing model in production?
Answer:
  "Sir, the system is protected by our MLflow Champion vs. Challenger Quality Gate (`promotion.py`). 
   The Challenger model is evaluated against the active Champion on a standardized, held-out benchmark test set. 
   The Challenger is ONLY promoted to 'Production' if its Macro F1 score exceeds the Champion's score by at least 0.01 (+1%). 
   If the retrained model performs worse or fails to meet the threshold, it is automatically rejected, 
   an alert is sent to engineers, and the existing Champion remains active in production without interruption."

Q8: How do Prometheus and Grafana communicate with your FastAPI serving application?
Answer:
  "Sir, Prometheus uses a 'PULL' (scrape) model. 
   Our FastAPI application exposes a `/metrics` endpoint using the official `prometheus_client` library. 
   Prometheus is configured in `prometheus.yml` to scrape `http://fastapi:8000/metrics` every 15 seconds. 
   Prometheus stores these metrics as time-series data. 
   Grafana connects to Prometheus as its data source, runs PromQL queries like `vulnerability_data_drift_share` 
   and `rate(vulnerability_prediction_requests_total[1m])`, and refreshes the visualization panels every 5 seconds."

Q9: What is the purpose of the Append-Only database design?
Answer:
  "Sir, in standard databases, when an EPSS score or KEV status is updated, the old row is overwritten. 
   However, in cybersecurity MLOps, knowing the exact historical state on a specific date is crucial. 
   By making `epss_history` and `kev_history` append-only, we preserve a complete audit trail. 
   When backtesting or retraining, we can query the exact threat intelligence available on March 15th, 2024, 
   ensuring that no future data leaks into the past."

Q10: Why did you use SMOTE? Did you apply SMOTE on the entire dataset or just the training set?
Answer:
  "Sir, software vulnerability severity is heavily imbalanced—there are many HIGH and CRITICAL vulnerabilities, 
   but very few NONE or LOW vulnerabilities. Without balancing, the model would be biased toward predicting HIGH. 
   We used SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) to synthesize realistic minority class samples. 
   Crucially, we applied SMOTE ONLY to the training split AFTER the temporal split was completed. 
   Applying SMOTE before splitting or to the test set would corrupt the evaluation and cause data leakage."
